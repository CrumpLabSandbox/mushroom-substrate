## 1. Summary

1. Fruiting is a switch from balanced growth to reserve mobilisation in a mycelium that is big enough and well stocked, not a starving one. Typical cues: nutrient supply falling, a temperature drop of 5 to 10 °C, lower CO2, blue light, high humidity [2].
2. Many primordia start (aggregates 100 to 200 µm across); few mature. Winners pull resources from the whole network; losers abort [2].
3. Expansion is hydraulic. A Coprinopsis stipe goes from about 20 mm to about 100 mm in under 12 h on about 200 mg of water, with almost no new dry mass [1][8].
4. A mushroom pushes with roughly 0.5 to 0.7 atm (50 to 70 kPa); Buller's ink cap lifted 200 g without slowing [7][8].
5. Stipes re-orient to vertical: reaction time about 25 min, about 51° per hour in Coprinopsis, about 9° per hour in Flammulina [1][9].
6. Ballistospores leave at about 0.5 to 1.8 m/s, at thousands of g, and stop within 0.004 to 1.8 mm. Range is set by spore and Buller's drop size and is matched to gill spacing [14][15][16].
7. One cap sheds on the order of 10^4 spores per second, 10^9 per day [17][18][25].
8. Caps cool themselves by evaporation (up to 4 °C) and make their own airflow of a few cm/s that carries spores out of 1 cm gaps [17].
9. Most spores land within about 1 m of the cap [20]; field fruiting tracks weather 10 to 30 days earlier, not the last 5 days [2].
10. No measured airborne sound from basidiospore discharge was found. Event rates and impact impulses exist and are usable as signals.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Triggers for fruiting

**General rule.** Fruiting follows nutrient depletion of the medium in vitro, but the mycelium is not starving: it has accumulated reserves and continues to grow to feed its fruit bodies. The trigger is the change from importing nutrients to mobilising reserves [2]. Glucose often represses fruiting even at low concentration; slowly hydrolysed carbon sources (oligo- and polysaccharides) favour it [2]. High carbon and nitrogen together block induction in many species [3].

**Minimum size and competence.** A mycelium must exceed a minimum size, possibly a minimum age, and hold enough reserves for the smallest viable fruit body. Buller made this argument for Coprinus sterquilinus [2]. Small species fruit on a leaf petiole; perennial brackets need a large domain of wood [2].

**Temperature.** Fruiting occurs over a narrower and usually lower temperature range than mycelial growth. Cultivated species commonly need a downshift of 5 to 10 °C plus lower CO2: Agaricus bisporus, Coprinopsis cinerea, Flammulina velutipes, Lentinula edodes, Pleurotus ostreatus and others are listed [2]. Kauserud et al. state that many mushrooms can be induced by a drop of at least 5 °C [5].

**CO2.** Elevated CO2 suppresses initiation in Schizophyllum commune. In Agaricus, CO2 lengthens the stem, while removing CO2 speeds cap and gill expansion and spore maturation; the suggested function is to lift the cap out of the CO2-rich layer at the substrate surface [2]. High CO2 suppresses cap differentiation in general [3].

**Light.** Near-UV (320 to 400 nm) and blue (400 to 520 nm) are the effective bands; some species need light and dark periods in sequence [2]. Some species fruit in the dark, but light speeds induction [3].

**Humidity and water.** High relative humidity usually favours initiation. Substrate water potential matters more: Pleurotus sajor-caju formed primordia at -2.5 MPa but not at -3.5 MPa, though it still grew [2]. Optimum pH for fruiting is 6 to 7 for several species, pH 4 for Lentinula edodes [2].

**Edges, injury and neighbours.** In culture, fruiting often starts where growth is checked: the dish edge, a barrier, a change in substrate, a wound, or a zone of interaction with another fungus [2]. Coprinus congregatus fruited from a much smaller resource volume when competing than when alone [2]. Many A. bisporus strains fruit only with bacteria (pseudomonads) that remove inhibitors [2].

**Species values.**

- *Coprinopsis cinerea.* Standard regime 25 °C, 90% RH, 12 h light / 12 h dark at 50 to 75 µE m^-2 s^-1. Hyphal knots form in the dark; light turns them into initials. Blue light (400 to 500 nm) is active; exposures as short as seconds can trigger steps. Glucose inhibits initiation. In the dark, knots become sclerotia (about 0.25 mm) instead, or stipes etiolate into "dark stipes" 20 cm or more long [1].
- *Agaricus bisporus.* Optimum for both mycelial growth and formation of initials is 24 °C, but initials stop at about 2 mm cap diameter unless temperature falls to about 16 °C [2]. Three separate controls: the volatile 1-octen-3-ol governs the step from hyphae to knots, a temperature drop is needed for primordia, and CO2 level sets how many fruit bodies develop [4].
- *Flammulina velutipes.* Initiates at continuous 20 °C or after 12 h at 15 °C [2]. Typical: growth 25 °C, fruiting 15 °C; some strains fruit at 22 °C; light of roughly 100 to 1,000 lux for 4 to 8 h per day helps primordia, and dark-grown fruit bodies are poor [28].
- *Pleurotus ostreatus.* Needs a cold shock. Reported practice: 22 to 24 °C incubation for about 20 days, then a drop of 8 to 10 °C; fruiting at about 18 to 20 °C, 80 to 95% RH, 12 h light; no primordia in the dark; very CO2-sensitive, with a commonly quoted limit below 1,000 ppm (one patent reports induction at 2,000 to 3,000 ppm) [29]. Low confidence: these come from search summaries of mixed-quality sources.
- *Lentinula edodes.* Temperature downshift listed as required [2]; fruiting optimum pH 4 [2]; cap expands by hyphal proliferation, not inflation [8]. I could not source induction temperatures or the cold-water soak in this pass.
- *Schizophyllum commune.* Blue light is required in the dikaryon (white-collar receptor complex); high CO2 represses fruiting through cAMP; lab phenotyping at 25 °C with 16 h light / 8 h dark [30]. Its natural logic: CO2 is high inside wood and normal at the surface, so mycelium reaching the surface becomes compact and competent [2].

**Wild phenology.** Most Basidiomycota fruit in autumn after spring and summer growth; temperature and rainfall are the main controls [2].

- In a 21-year Swiss plot, a 1 °C rise in July to August temperature delayed saprotroph fruiting by about 7 days, and productivity tracked June to October precipitation [2].
- In Mediterranean oak forest, weather in the 5 days before a survey had little effect, but weather 10 to 30 days before did [2].
- Norway, 1940 to 2006, about 34,500 herbarium records: average delay of 12.9 days since 1980, strongest in early-fruiting species, so the season compressed into late autumn. Fruiting is 10 to 20 days earlier in northern, continental and alpine areas [5].
- Southern England, 1950 to 2005, 200 species: the season lengthened; among species with significant shifts, first fruiting advanced 7.9 days per decade and last fruiting moved 7.2 days later per decade; some autumn species now also fruit in spring [2].
- Across four European countries the season widened between 1970 and 2007 [6].

### 2.2 Development

**Stages (Coprinopsis cinerea, the best-timed case) [1].**

| Stage | Size | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Primary hyphal knot (dark) | about 0.2 mm | forms on competent mycelium |
| Secondary knot / initial (light) | 0.2 to 0.5 mm core, up to 2 mm | day 0 |
| First tissue differentiation | 0.4 to 0.7 mm | |
| Mushroom shape visible | 0.8 to 1.0 mm | |
| Stage 1 primordium | 2 to 3 mm growing to 7 to 9 mm | days 1 to 2; prefusion phase about 24 h |
| Stage 2 primordium, karyogamy | 6 to 10 mm (up to 15) | day 3 |
| Meiosis, sporulation | 10 to 45 mm | stage 2 to mature takes about 24 h |
| Rapid stipe elongation and cap opening | 80 mm of elongation | starts about 8 h after karyogamy; under 12 h |
| Autolysis | | begins about 7 h after cap opening starts |

Initial to mature fruit body takes 4 to 5 days; the whole life cycle fits in 2 weeks [1]. A cap holds 10^7 to 10^8 meiotic cells [1], so 4 x 10^7 to 4 x 10^8 spores (derived, four per basidium).

**Aggregation.** Initials are tufts of 100 to 200 µm where branches switch from avoiding to seeking each other. They form in large numbers across the colony; only a few continue and become sinks for translocation [2]. In Coprinus lagopus, the two halves of an intact colony differed in fruit body biomass by up to 10:1, but halves cut apart early yielded equally: resources flow to favoured primordia [2].

**Expansion.** Elongation is by cell inflation with constant turgor and unthickened walls: a hydrostatic skeleton. An average C. cinerea stem grows from about 20 mm to about 100 mm, doubling fresh weight with almost no change in dry weight, taking in nearly 200 mg of water [8]. Urea (Coprinopsis) or mannitol (Agaricus, Lentinula) is the osmoticum. Agaricus and Coprinopsis caps expand by inflation; Lentinula and Schizophyllum by proliferation [8]. Derived rate for Coprinopsis: 80 mm in under 12 h is at least 6.7 mm/h, about 1.9 µm/s.

**Force.** Buller loaded Coprinus sterquilinus with lead shot: at 150 g it still grew about 1 mm in 2 h, 200 g did not slow it, and the stem buckled at 300 g; he gave the pressure as at least two thirds of an atmosphere [8]. Strain-gauge measurements on C. cinerea give about 0.5 atm, the same as its vegetative hyphae [7]. Kües quotes a stipe osmotic value of 0.45 to 0.5 M and about 7 x 10^4 N/m^2 [1]. Derived: a 5 mm stipe at 67 kPa pushes with about 1.3 N. A much lower figure of 1.33 kPa is quoted for Phallus impudicus breaking asphalt [23]; I only saw that second-hand and it does not sit easily with the others.

**Resource cost.** Supplying the nitrogen for 1 g of Ganoderma applanatum fruit body takes all the nitrogen in 13.6 g of wood; 1 g of spores takes 36.1 g. Fomes fomentarius shed 1.115 g of spores in 20 days. A 1 kg bracket therefore draws on more than 14 kg of wood [2]. No comparable carbon budget for an agaric was found in this pass.

**Where on the network.** At checks to growth (edges, barriers, substrate boundaries, wounds, interaction zones), on surface or aerial mycelium where CO2 is low, and close together when a sink has formed (clumps) [2]. Crusts fruit over the whole exposed surface and merge on contact [2].

**Tropisms.** In C. cinerea a 7 to 10 min exposure is enough to sense a change in gravity, visible bending starts at about 25 min, proceeds at about 51° per hour and is complete in 2 to 3 h. Flammulina responds at about 9° per hour and takes about 12 h; its sensing zone is the few mm under the cap and bending occurs over up to 2 cm below [1][9]. Early primordia and dark stipes grow toward light; mature stipes follow gravity [1]. Gills must hang vertical for spores to fall clear [9]; I found no kinetics for gill gravitropism.

**Lifespan.** A few days to weeks for fleshy fruit bodies, years for perennial brackets [2]. Ink cap caps digest themselves within hours of opening [1].

### 2.3 Shape and morphogenesis models

**Neighbour-Sensing model (Meškauskas, McNulty, Fricker, Moore).** A vector model in which every tip grows in a direction computed from fields generated by the rest of the mycelium: a short-range density field (negative autotropism), a longer-range secondary autotropism, a galvanotropic field parallel to hyphae, and a gravitropic term with a settable angle (0 to 90°), strength (0 to 1) and a turn that depends on distance from the root. Branching can be gated by the local density field (for example branch only if the field is below 0.1, 0.05 or 0.01) or by neighbour count [10]. Fruit-body-like forms arise from applying the same parameter change to all tips at once, with no global plan [10]:

- gravitropic angle of 45° plus negative autotropism gives a hollow cone, that is a cup;
- positive long-range autotropism after a threshold distance closes the cup into a sphere;
- 100 time units flat (90°), then 200 units rotating toward vertical gives a cup; raising then lowering long-range autotropism closes it and gives a bottle-shaped stem; a late change in autotropism and gravitropism makes tips cascade down the outside as a cap.

The intermediate shapes resemble cup, closed and flask-shaped fruit bodies of other groups [10]. The companion paper adds a horizontal-plane tropism for flat colonies [11]. The gravitropic term is an abstract orientation field and could equally be light or a chemical gradient [10].

**Spore-launch geometry as a shape constraint.** Discharge range must be shorter than the gap to the opposite gill face [14][15]. Iapichino et al. predict that maximum spore packing needs gill spacing, spore radius and drop radius to be co-ordinated; with drop radius proportional to spore radius, intergill distance scales as spore radius to the power 3/2, and the drop is about half the linear size of the spore [16]. Across 382 pored species, tube radius correlated only weakly with spore volume (r^2 = 0.098) [14].

**Not found.** I did not find, in this pass, a published mechanical model of cap expansion or a lateral-inhibition model of gill spacing. From memory (unverified): Moore's group described gill number increasing as the cap expands, by new gills arising where neighbours have moved apart, which is behaviourally a spacing rule. Treat any such rule in the simulator as inferred.

### 2.4 Hyphal systems

Unverified, from memory of Corner's scheme; not checked in this pass. Monomitic fruit bodies contain only generative hyphae (thin-walled, septate) and are soft and short-lived, like most agarics. Dimitic ones add skeletal hyphae (thick-walled, unbranched) or binding hyphae; trimitic ones have all three and are tough and perennial, like many brackets. For the model this maps to one number, toughness or persistence, that sets lifespan and stiffness.

### 2.5 Spore production and release

**Output.** A gilled mushroom can release about 30,000 spores per second, billions per day [18]. Buller's Agaricus campestris estimate was 1.8 x 10^9 spores over two days, 40 million per hour [25]; Money gives 2.7 x 10^9 per day, 31,000 per second [25]. Measured flux from 24 Pleurotus ostreatus caps: 540 ± 490 spores per cm^2 per second [17]; for a 50 cm^2 underside that is 27,000 per second (derived), which agrees. Hericium erinaceus sporulation peaks near midday, rises from 0 °C to a maximum at 24 to 27 °C and stops at 31 to 33 °C [2]. Ganoderma applanatum is widely quoted at 30 billion per day for months, but only from secondary sources [26]. A giant puffball holds "several trillion" [24].

**Ballistospore mechanism.** A drop (Buller's drop) condenses at the base of the spore on secreted sugars, grows over seconds, then merges with a film on the spore face. The released surface energy shifts the centre of mass and throws spore and drop off the sterigma [12][13][18].

- Launch speed: 0.1 to 1.8 m/s across species; species means 0.53 to 1.42 m/s [14][15].
- Acceleration: 32,400 m/s^2 (Auricularia) to 140,000 m/s^2 (Tilletia), about 3,300 to 14,000 g [14]; earlier figures of over 10,000 g and 25,000 g [12][27].
- Energy transfer completes in under 4 µs over about 5 µm; predicted 1.2 m/s against observed 0.8 m/s for Auricularia [13].
- Range: 0.004 mm (Hyphodontia latitans) to 1.83 mm (Aleurodiscus gigasporus), 1 to 63 spore lengths; gilled species under 0.1 mm, exposed-surface species 1 to 2 mm [14][15].
- Buller's drop radius: 0.3 to 8.3 µm; drop growth 2 to 3 s in Agaricus bisporus and yeasts, almost 1 min for the largest; growth time scales roughly as radius to the fourth power [15].
- Energies: kinetic 3.4 x 10^-16 to 2.7 x 10^-12 J per spore; about 3 to 9% of surface energy becomes kinetic; break-off force averages 0.15 µN [15].
- Spore mass: 0.6 pg to 17 ng [15]; a typical spore is about 5 x 10^-13 kg [17].

A usable rule (derived, Stokes drag): range = v0 x tau, with tau = 2 rho r^2 / (9 eta). For r = 4 µm, tau is about 0.24 ms, so 1 m/s gives 0.24 mm. This matches the measured values [14].

**After launch.** Spores fall at 1 to 4 mm/s [17]. Evaporation from a cap (3 x 10^-5 kg m^-2 s^-1, about 70 W/m^2) cools the cap by up to 4 °C and the gap air by 1 to 2 °C. The denser air flows out as a gravity current of about 4 cm/s from a 1 cm gap. Reach is d = U h / v_s and grows as the square of gap height; it does not depend on cap diameter or spore rate. Flow leaves in one or two tongues because the cap is asymmetric. At a barrier the current climbs and an eddy lifts spores 10 cm or more. Outside wind cannot enter gaps narrower than about 6 to 20 mm [17]. Mushrooms lose water faster than any plant tested, and twice as fast when attached to mycelium [17].

**Deposition.** Slides on 60 cm transects around ectomycorrhizal mushrooms fitted a negative exponential; 95% of spores fell within about 1 m (58 cm by one secondary summary), further for taller caps and smaller spores [20]. I did not open the long-distance kernel studies (Norros and others); the tail is unquantified here.

**Puffballs.** A raindrop of 1 mm or more on Lycoperdon perlatum ejects over a million spores at about 100 cm/s, forming a cloud about 1 cm tall within 0.01 s [21] (Gregory 1949, seen only via Wikipedia).

**Bird's nest fungi.** Raindrops splash peridioles out of Cyathus and Crucibulum cups at 67 to 73° to an estimated 1 m; a cord then tethers them to vegetation. Nidularia throws them sideways at a mean 1.2 m/s [22].

**Stinkhorns.** Phallus impudicus egg 4 to 6 x 3 to 5 cm; stalk 10 to 30 cm, expanding in a day or two (10 to 15 cm per hour is quoted without a source); blow-flies eat the spore slime and excrete spores soon after leaving [23].

**Cannons, for comparison.** Pressurised cells at 0.3 to 0.55 MPa: Ascobolus immersus 5 to 18 m/s, 1.8 x 10^6 m/s^2, 0.3 m; Podospora anserina 10 to 25 m/s, 0.2 m; Pilobolus kleinii 2 to 13 m/s, 2.1 x 10^5 m/s^2, 2.5 m, discharge lasting about 0.8 ms; Basidiobolus 2 to 9 m/s, 0.02 m [19]. Synchronised ascospore puffs drag air along and extend range (Roper 2010; unverified, known to me only through [17]).

### 2.6 Sound and vibration

- **Measured (mechanical, not airborne sound):** Pilobolus sporangia were timed with phonograph-cartridge transducers triggered by discharge and by impact, giving 4.7 to 27.5 m/s (mean 10.8 m/s) [32]. So one launch and one landing are each detectable as a mechanical impulse.
- **Not found:** any microphone recording or sound-pressure figure for ballistospore discharge, puffing, or mushroom expansion.
- **Anecdotal (unverified, from memory of Buller):** audible puffing of cup fungi and the tap of Pilobolus sporangia on glass.
- **Speculative (derived):** 30,000 launches per second at about 10^-13 J each is about 3 nW of mechanical power per cap, far too little to hear. As a signal it is a dense random pulse train with a daily envelope; raindrop-driven puffs and splashes are sparse, loud events by comparison.

## 5. What this means for the simulator

Simplest first. Each rule names the tier where it becomes affordable.

1. **Fruiting gate (4K, 40K).** Fruit when network reserve exceeds a species threshold AND local nutrient supply is falling AND temperature has dropped by at least 5 °C from its recent average AND humidity is high. One threshold number is enough at 4K.
2. **Weather memory (40K).** Use weather averaged over the previous 10 to 30 days, not the current day [2]. Shift onset by about 7 days per °C of late-summer warmth [2].
3. **Placement (40K).** Score candidate nodes: surface or near-surface, low CO2, at a substrate boundary, obstacle, wound or interaction zone, and near the reserve-rich part of the network [2].
4. **Many start, few finish (40K).** Spawn many 100 to 200 µm initials. Each matures only if it wins a sink competition; the winners drain neighbours, and nearby winners form a clump. Abort the rest and return their mass [2].
5. **Cost (40K).** Charge the network dry mass and nitrogen per fruit body; Ganoderma's 13.6 g of wood per gram of bracket is the only sourced budget [2]. Expansion itself costs water, not dry mass [8].
6. **Two-phase growth (400K).** Slow primordium growth over days with full dry-mass cost, then fast inflation over hours that needs water: Coprinopsis 80 mm in 12 h on 200 mg of water [1][8]. No water, no expansion.
7. **Environment sculpts shape (400K).** High CO2: longer stem, smaller cap [2]. No light: no cap, long thin stem toward any light [1]. This gives shape variation from one parameter set.
8. **Orientation (400K).** Stem axis relaxes toward vertical with a 25 min delay at 9 to 51° per hour [1][9]; gills and pores hang vertical.
9. **Force (400K).** Expansion stalls if the load over the tip area exceeds 50 to 70 kPa [1][7][8]; otherwise soil and litter are pushed aside.
10. **Lifespan (400K).** Toughness sets persistence: hours (ink cap), days to weeks (fleshy), years (bracket) [1][2].
11. **Spore output (4M).** Rate equals fertile area times about 500 spores per cm^2 per second [17], scaled by a temperature curve (peak 24 to 27 °C, zero above about 32 °C [2]) and a humidity gate.
12. **Launch (4M).** Each spore gets v0 of 0.5 to 1.5 m/s normal to the gill; range is v0 x tau from spore radius; require range less than half the gill gap [14][15][16]. This also sets gill spacing from spore size.
13. **Under-cap airflow (4M).** Cap temperature drops with evaporation; outflow speed about 4 cm/s x (gap / 1 cm); reach d = U h / v_s; send it out in one or two tongues [17].
14. **Deposition (4M, and the lite block).** Negative exponential kernel with 95% inside 1 m [20], plus a small escape fraction handed to the wind.
15. **Other release modes (4M).** Raindrop events drive puffs (10^6 spores at 1 m/s per hit [21]) and splashes (peridioles at about 70°, up to 1 m [22]); stinkhorns hand spores to a fly agent [23].
16. **Growing the shape from tips (4M, optional).** The Neighbour-Sensing sequence (flat, cup, close, stem, cap) is a recipe for growing a primordium from the same tip rules as the network, by scheduling changes to a gravitropic angle and an attraction strength [10].
17. **Signals.** Publish events: initial formed, primordium aborted, expansion start, spore launch (as a rate), raindrop puff, splash, autolysis.

## 6. Open or contested points

- **Coprinopsis and 37 °C.** One reading of Kües gave light-induced fruiting across 20 to 37 °C; the usual lab practice is growth at 37 °C and fruiting at 25 to 28 °C [1]. Needs a direct check of the text.
- **CO2 thresholds.** No primary source with ppm values was opened. The 1,000 ppm figure for Pleurotus is trade practice and is contradicted by one patent [29].
- **Mushroom force.** 50 to 70 kPa for Coprinus [1][7][8] against 1.33 kPa quoted for Phallus [23]. The Phallus figure was not checked at source.
- **Phenology direction.** Norway shows later and compressed seasons [5]; England shows longer seasons in both directions [2]. Gange et al. also disputed the climate attribution in [5] (seen in a search summary only).
- **Acceleration figures** vary by a factor of about 8 between papers and species [12][14][27]. Yafetto's abstract (20,000 to 180,000 g) and table do not line up exactly for Pilobolus [19].
- **95% within 1 m** comes from 60 cm transects and a fitted model [20]; the long tail is real but unquantified here.
- **Ganoderma 30 billion spores per day** has no primary source that I could find [26]. The "7 x 10^18" giant puffball figure on Wikipedia is not credible; "several trillion" is the usable statement [24].
- **Gill spacing by lateral inhibition, mechanical cap models:** none found; anything of that kind in the simulator is our own construction.
- **Hyphal systems, Buller's sound anecdotes, Roper 2010, Trail 2005:** from memory, not opened.
- **Lentinula edodes** induction temperatures, **Agaricus** flush timing and **stinkhorn** expansion rates were not sourced.
- **Sound:** no acoustic measurement found. Absence here reflects a limited search, not a settled negative.
